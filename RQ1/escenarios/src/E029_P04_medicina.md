## PROGRESSION:ADVANCES
We are given: "A 60-year-old man comes to the emergency department 40 minutes after the onset of crushing substernal chest pain radiating to his jaw. He is diaphoretic and nauseated. An ECG shows 3-mm ST-segment elevations in leads II, III, and aVF with reciprocal ST depression in leads I and aVL. Occlusion of which of the following vessels is the most likely cause?"

We need to localize the infarction from the ECG and then name the artery.

## PROGRESSION:ADVANCES
Clinical clues: acute crushing chest pain with diaphoresis and nausea, and ST elevation with reciprocal changes. This is an ST-elevation myocardial infarction. The leads with ST elevation tell us which wall is infarcted.

## ERROR
ST elevation in leads II, III, and aVF indicates an anterior wall infarction, so the occluded vessel is the left anterior descending artery.

## VERIFICATION:REFUTED | implicita
Leads II, III, and aVF look at the inferior surface of the heart from below; the anterior wall is seen in the precordial leads V1 through V4, none of which shows ST elevation here, and the reciprocal depression in I and aVL is the mirror of an inferior injury, not an anterior one.

## CORRECTION:RECOVERED | implicita
The infarction is inferior, and the inferior wall is supplied by the right coronary artery in the great majority of people.

## PROGRESSION:ADVANCES
The right coronary artery gives off the posterior descending artery in a right-dominant circulation, which is present in about 85 percent of people, and the posterior descending artery supplies the inferior wall of the left ventricle.

## SUPPORT
Inferior infarctions often cause nausea and diaphoresis through vagal activation, because the inferior wall of the left ventricle is rich in vagal afferent fibers.

## PROGRESSION:REACHES_OUTPUT
So the most likely occluded vessel is the right coronary artery, option C.

Thus final answer: C.
