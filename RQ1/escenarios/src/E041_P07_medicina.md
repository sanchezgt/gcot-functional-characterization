## PROGRESSION:ADVANCES
Okay, let's see. A 62-year-old man with epigastric pain radiating to the back, weight loss, new-onset diabetes, painless jaundice, and a palpable nontender gallbladder, with a direct hyperbilirubinemia and high alkaline phosphatase. The question asks for the next step to establish the diagnosis and determine resectability.

## PROGRESSION:ADVANCES
Painless obstructive jaundice with a palpable nontender gallbladder, weight loss, back pain, and recent diabetes in a man in his sixties is the classic presentation of carcinoma of the head of the pancreas obstructing the common bile duct.

## PROGRESSION:ABANDONED
My first thought for the next step is an abdominal ultrasound, since it is the usual first test for jaundice: it would show dilated bile ducts and perhaps a mass, and it is cheap and quick. So I start down that path: order the ultrasound, and if it shows ductal dilation, proceed from there.

## ALTERNATIVE:ADOPTED | explicita
Alternatively, a contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen both demonstrates the pancreatic mass and shows its relationship to the vessels and any metastases, which is exactly what "establish the diagnosis and determine resectability" asks for. Given how high the pretest probability already is, the ultrasound would only delay that answer, so I'll switch to the CT as the next step and drop the ultrasound-first plan.

## PROGRESSION:ADVANCES
A pancreatic-protocol CT with contrast is the standard test for staging a suspected pancreatic cancer: it defines the mass, its contact with the superior mesenteric vessels, and liver or peritoneal spread, which together decide whether a Whipple resection is possible.

## SUPPORT
If the CT shows a resectable mass, the patient can go to surgery without a prior tissue diagnosis; if it shows unresectable disease, biopsy and biliary stenting follow.

## PROGRESSION:REACHES_OUTPUT
So the answer should be B, contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen.
